\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Abstract}\quad
We model choices and reaction times of 49 participants in a probability discounting task with
reward and loss conditions. One valuation model is fixed: a Rachlin hyperboloid
$V_i=r_i/(1+h\theta_i)$ on both options, separate gain and loss rates, with a logistic
choice rule on the normalised value difference $\Delta V$. Three RT models differ only in
how $\Delta V$ sets the location of a shifted log-normal: linear conflict ($|\Delta V|$), where
value distance slows the choice; decision uncertainty ($|2P-1|$), where indecision does; and
quadratic conflict ($\Delta V^2$), where only near-ties slow it. Run A is sure-vs-risky, run B
risky-vs-risky: an unseen structure, not a retest. Estimation is per-participant MAP on run A.
Every parameter recovers well ($r=0.83$--$0.98$) except non-decision time ($r\le0.29$). On the run B RT predictive log-likelihood, decision uncertainty wins ($-1.492$ nats
per trial against $-1.536$ and $-1.562$; $p=0.040$, $0.004$), the only ranking that
replicates in-sample. Adding RT sharpens recovery of the discount rates (RMSE ratio
$0.47$, quadratic conflict), but this does not transfer: against a choice-only baseline it
moves held-out choice prediction by at most $0.037$ nats per trial, none significant, and
cross-context reliability of the discount parameters is near zero. A same-task control locates
the cause: held-out run A choices reach $-0.464$ nats per trial, 49 of 49 above
chance, against $-1.004$ and 6 of 49 across tasks. We recommend decision
uncertainty as the best description of response time; modelling RT does not improve the account
of choices.

\section{Introduction}

Every trial of a value-based choice task yields two observations: which option was taken, and
how long the participant took to take it. Discounting research almost always uses only the
first, because discount rates are traditionally estimated from titrated indifference points,
which summarise a block of choices and discard their timing, and because discounting models are
static value functions with no dimension along which a response time could be predicted
\citep{green2004}. Both modalities are nevertheless driven by the same latent
quantity: if subjective value drives the choice, it should also govern how long the comparison
takes, making RT a second and essentially free measurement of the same construct. If RTs carry
information about $h$ and $\beta$, fitting both should sharpen those estimates; if not, the
joint fit pays for the RT term and gets nothing back.

We therefore hold valuation and choice entirely fixed, using a Rachlin hyperboloid in odds
against \citep{rachlin1991} with a logistic choice rule, and vary only the value\,$\to$\,RT
mapping: linear conflict (M1), the first-order behaviour of an accumulator with drift
proportional to $\Delta V$ \citep{ratcliff1978,ratcliff2008}; decision uncertainty (M2), which
saturates once a participant is confident; and quadratic conflict (M3), which concentrates the
slowing near indifference. The three span the plausible curvatures, and the report asks in turn
which mapping predicts RT best out of sample, whether its parameters are identifiable and
reliable, and whether modelling RT improves the account of the choices.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data}

The probability discounting data set contains 49 participants, each with an inference run (A)
and an evaluation run (B) of 200 trials. On each trial a smaller reward $r_{cert}$ is offered
against a larger reward $r_{unc}$ paid with probability
$p\in\{0.10,0.25,0.50,0.75,0.90\}$, which we summarise by the odds against,
$\theta=(1-p)/p$. A reward condition (both magnitudes positive) and a loss condition (both
negative, verified in the raw files, so no sign correction is applied anywhere) are fully
crossed with the design at 100 trials per condition per run.

\emph{The two runs are not the same task.} \texttt{p\_cert} is absent for every run A trial and
present for every run B trial ($0.3$--$0.7$), in all 49 participants: run A is sure-vs-risky
($\theta_{cert}=0$), run B risky-vs-risky ($\theta_{cert}\in[0.43,2.33]$). The
\texttt{cert}/\texttt{unc} labels are inherited from run A, but what the runs share is
magnitude, not certainty. Section~2.2 discounts both options with one equation, and the runs are
read as two decision contexts, not two administrations of one task.

We drop 68 non-responses (0.35\%) and 105 trials faster than 250\,ms (0.54\%), a fixed global
floor for anticipatory responses, leaving 19{,}427 of 19{,}600. No upper cut-off is applied:
neither extreme (200\,ms, 9996\,ms) shows a pile-up, so there is no sign of censoring. Four
participants make more than 10\% of their responses below 400\,ms; we flag and keep them. Participants choose the uncertain option on 46.4\% of reward and 43.2\% of
loss trials (between-participant SDs $0.14$, $0.10$), respond more slowly under losses (median
2.09\,s) than gains (1.81\,s), and differ enormously in speed (participant median RTs span
0.40--3.50\,s). Raw RT is right-skewed (1.55) and near-symmetric on the log scale ($-0.51$),
which is what recommends a log-normal observation model.

Before fitting we check the valuation model model-free. A logistic of
$\log(r_{unc}/r_{cert})$ fitted at each $p$ and condition gives the indifference ratio at that
$\theta$, and a hyperboloid predicts $r_{unc}/r_{cert}=1+h\theta$, linear in $\theta$; the
rearrangement presupposes an undiscounted smaller option, so it is estimated on run A only.
Figure~\ref{fig:data} shows that it holds, with slopes $h\approx3.72$ for gains and
$h\approx0.64$ for losses, the model-free signature of the reflection effect
\citep{kahneman1979,green2004}, and close to the fitted medians below, which is the check that
the likelihood is coded correctly.

The runs also differ in coverage and behaviour: run B's offers move to larger magnitude ratios
(mean log ratio $1.73$ against $1.32$), 23\% of its trials fall outside the participant's own
run A range, the uncertain option is chosen more often (0.394 to 0.503, $p=0.001$; a residual
$+0.038$ survives matching participant, condition, $p$ and ratio octile), and responses speed up
sharply (median 2.19\,s to 1.77\,s, 45 of 49 faster) while Section~2.3 fixes $\tau$ and $a$ from
run A. Section~3.2 therefore measures generalisation across decision contexts rather than
test--retest, and Section~3.3 extrapolation to an unseen structure; Section~2.4 adds two
controls that keep those readings separable.

\subsection{Models}

\paragraph{Valuation and choice (held fixed).}
Both options are discounted by the odds against their own outcome \citep{rachlin1991}:
\begin{equation}
V_i=\frac{r_i}{1+h\,\theta_i},\qquad \theta_i=\frac{1-p_i}{p_i},\qquad h\ge0,
\end{equation}
with separate rates $h_{gain}$ and $h_{loss}$ per participant. One equation covers both runs,
reducing exactly to $V_{cert}=r_{cert}$ where $\theta_{cert}=0$; hard-coding that instead would
overstate the smaller option by a factor $1+h\theta_{cert}$, between $1.3$ and $8.6$ at the
fitted rates, on every one of the 9{,}700 retained run B trials. Because loss magnitudes are
stored negative, the same equation produces both risk attitudes at no extra parameter cost:
$V_{unc}$ shrinks towards zero, worse than a sure gain (risk aversion) but better than a sure
loss (risk seeking), so the reflection effect emerges for free.
Magnitudes span five orders of magnitude, so we normalise,
$\Delta V=(V_{unc}-V_{cert})/[(|r_{cert}|+|r_{unc}|)/2]$, which lets one sensitivity serve small
and large trials but removes absolute magnitude effects. Choice is
logistic, $P_t\equiv P(\text{uncertain})=\sigma(\beta\Delta V_t+\beta_0)$, where $\beta_0$
absorbs a value-independent bias, giving the Bernoulli trial likelihood
$\log p(y_t\mid\theta)=y_t\log P_t+(1-y_t)\log(1-P_t)$ with $y_t=1$ when the uncertain option
was chosen.

\paragraph{RT distribution (held fixed).}
RTs follow a shifted log-normal, $RT=\tau+\exp(\mu+\sigma\varepsilon)$ with
$\varepsilon\sim\cN(0,1)$, so for $rt>\tau$
\begin{equation}
\log p(rt\mid\mu,\sigma,\tau)=-\log(rt-\tau)-\log\sigma-\tfrac12\log2\pi
-\tfrac12\Big[\tfrac{\log(rt-\tau)-\mu}{\sigma}\Big]^2 .
\end{equation}
Holding this distribution identical across the three RT models is what makes the comparison one
of mappings rather than of distributional shape. The full trial likelihood is
$p(\text{choice},RT\mid\theta)=p(y_t\mid\theta)\,p(rt_t\mid\theta)$, since the modalities are
conditionally independent given the shared latent $\Delta V$; that shared valuation is what
makes the model multimodal. The models differ only in the location:
M1, $\mu_t=a-b\,|\Delta V_t|$; M2, $\mu_t=a-b\,|2P_t-1|$; M3, $\mu_t=a-b\,\Delta V_t^{2}$.

\paragraph{Psychological hypotheses.}
M1 says what slows a participant is the distance between the options in value space, every
extra unit of advantage buying the same speed, the first-order behaviour of an accumulator with
drift proportional to $\Delta V$ \citep{ratcliff2008,krajbich2010}. M2 says the driver is
subjective indecision: its predictor is $0$ under complete indecision and $1$ under certainty,
so once $P\approx0.95$ further advantage buys no more speed. M1 has RT tracking value, M2 has
it tracking confidence. M3 says deliberation is costly only very near indifference, so the
marginal speed-up grows rather than shrinks with $|\Delta V|$. The psychological claim in each
case is the curvature.

\paragraph{Choice-only baseline.}
The baseline deletes the RT term from the likelihood and fits the identical valuation model and
choice rule to the choices alone: not a fourth model but the same model fitted to one modality,
and the reference against which the RT modality is valued.

\subsection{Inference}

We estimate parameters per participant by maximum a posteriori on run A, leaving run B
untouched at this stage:
\begin{equation}
\log p(\theta\mid\text{data})\;\propto\;
\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_t\big[y_t\log P_t+(1-y_t)\log(1-P_t)\big]}_{\text{choice}}
\;+\;\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_t\log p(rt_t\mid\mu_t,\sigma,\tau)}_{\text{RT; omitted in the baseline}}
\;+\;\log p(\theta).
\end{equation}
Four choice parameters are fitted unconstrained on transformed scales
(\texttt{log\_h\_gain}, \texttt{log\_h\_loss}, \texttt{log\_beta}, \texttt{beta0}), and the RT
models add \texttt{a}, \texttt{b}, \texttt{log\_sigma} and \texttt{tau\_raw}, where
$\tau=\sigma(\texttt{tau\_raw})\cdot0.99\min(RT)$ enforces $0<\tau<\min(RT)$. Priors are weakly
informative Gaussians on the fitted scale (Table~\ref{tab:priors}), wide enough that the data
dominate wherever the likelihood has curvature; $b$'s is centred at zero, so a positive
difficulty effect is never assumed, and is widened to $\cN(0,3^2)$ for M3, whose predictor is on
a smaller scale (median $0.08$ against $0.28$), which equalises regularisation in effect on
$\mu$ rather than in raw coefficient.

Optimisation uses L-BFGS-B with box constraints and 10 restarts per participant, one from the
model-free estimates and the rest from the priors; seeds derive deterministically from (model,
run, participant), so the pipeline reproduces exactly. Convergence is reported for every run A
fit and every run B fit except M2 (98\%). Two diagnostics guard the estimates
(Fig.~\ref{fig:insample}): refitting by MLE returns essentially identical parameters for
well-identified participants ($r\ge0.995$), so the priors are not doing the work; and a
curvature check flags 19 of 49 participants as poorly identified in at least one parameter (14
in $\log h_{gain}$, 14 in $\log h_{loss}$), which we keep and track separately.

\subsection{Evaluation Plan}

\emph{Recovery.} For each RT model we draw 200 parameter sets covering the run A estimates,
simulate choices and RTs over a real participant's design, and refit twice, with the joint and
with the choice-only likelihood; correlation and RMSE quantify recovery, and the error
correlations diagnose identifiability. The study is then repeated over run B
designs, to ask whether $h$ is recoverable \emph{in principle} where $\theta_{cert}>0$,
separately from whether it transfers.
\emph{Reliability.} The same model is estimated independently on runs A and B and the estimates
correlated across participants (Pearson, Spearman, ICC(2,1) \citep{shrout1979}) with
2000-sample bootstrap CIs; CIs for differences apply one participant resample to both models,
preserving the pairing.
\emph{Prediction.} Run A parameters are applied unchanged to run B, nothing refitted; for choice
we report predictive log-likelihood per trial, accuracy, balanced accuracy and AUC, for RT
predictive log-likelihood per trial and RMSE on $\log(RT-\tau)$, each for all trials and by
condition.
\emph{Which quantity compares what.} A joint and a choice-only likelihood describe different
data, so their totals are not comparable: RT models are compared with each other on the RT
predictive log-likelihood only, RT-informed fits with the baseline on choice quantities only, by
paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.
\emph{Same-task control.} Because run B is a different task, a weak run A\,$\to$\,run B result is
ambiguous between a poor model and a limit on extrapolation, so we also fit each participant's
choice model on half of run A and predict the other half: held-out trials, same estimator, same
structure on both sides.

\section{Results}

The fits agree with the model-free analysis. Median run A baseline estimates are
$h_{gain}=2.65$ (IQR $1.00$--$7.99$), $h_{loss}=0.82$ ($0.21$--$1.98$), $\beta=4.53$ and
$\beta_0=-0.24$, with $h_{gain}>h_{loss}$ in 32 of 49 participants: the reflection effect is
recovered by the fit as well as by the raw indifference points, and both rates land near the
model-free $3.72$ and $0.64$.

\subsection{Parameter Recovery and Identifiability}

Every parameter but one recovers well under every RT model, with $r$ between $0.83$ and $0.98$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery}, Table~\ref{tab:recovery}). Non-decision time is the exception. $\tau$ is not identifiable, with $r=0.19$
(M1), $0.21$ (M2) and $0.29$ (M3) and an upward bias of about $+0.05$\,s, and the recovery
errors show why: $\mathrm{corr}(\text{err}_\tau,\text{err}_a)=-0.64$ under M1 and M3. Both shift
the predicted RT distribution and at $\approx200$ trials the data cannot separate them, so an
over-estimated shift is paid for by an under-estimated intercept. Nothing else trades off
appreciably (off-diagonal $|r|\le0.31$, Fig.~\ref{fig:recmatrix}).

Adding RT improves recovery of the choice parameters in every case, by amounts that differ
enormously between mappings. As an RMSE ratio (joint over choice-only, below 1 meaning RT
helps), M3 gives $0.47$ for $\log h_{gain}$ and $0.49$ for $\log h_{loss}$, roughly halving the
error, against $0.86$ and $0.72$ for M1 and $0.98$ and $0.88$ for M2; paired Wilcoxon tests on
absolute errors confirm the improvement under M1 and M3 ($p<10^{-4}$) and only weakly under M2
($p=0.03$ and $0.16$).

Repeating the study over run B designs answers a prior question that Section~3.3 cannot: is $h$
recoverable at all where $\theta_{cert}>0$? It is, though less sharply: $\log h_{gain}$ falls
from $0.925/0.900/0.977$ to $0.888/0.816/0.931$ with RMSE up by roughly $40\%$, as expected when
the identifying signal is split across two entangled discount terms instead of one, and M3's
advantage survives (RMSE ratio $0.61$ against $0.79$ and $1.01$). Whatever fails between the
runs in Section~3.2, it is not identifiability.

\subsection{Reliability}

Because run A and run B are different decision structures, this section measures whether a
construct generalises across contexts, not classical test--retest, and on that reading the
discounting parameters do not generalise (Fig.~\ref{fig:reliability},
Table~\ref{tab:reliability}): under the baseline $\log h_{gain}$ reaches only $r=0.180$ (CI
$[-0.08,+0.45]$, ICC $0.14$), $\log h_{loss}$ $0.191$, $\log\beta$ $0.215$ and $\beta_0$
$0.103$, every CI including zero. Such values are low even by the standards of cognitive-task
parameters \citep{hedge2018,enkavi2019}; Section~3.3 shows why.

What \emph{is} stable is everything independent of the valuation: the RT level $a$, $\tau$
and $\log\sigma$ (for which we report Spearman, since a few extreme-RT participants inflate
Pearson). The difficulty slope $b$ is not stable at all. Participants carry a
reproducible speed and spread between the two tasks, but neither a reproducible discount rate
nor a reproducible sensitivity of RT to difficulty.

Adding RT does not repair this, and twice makes it worse. Relative to the baseline, $\Delta r$
for $\log h_{gain}$ is $-0.056$ (M1, CI $[-0.30,+0.17]$), $+0.049$ (M2, $[-0.16,+0.28]$) and
$-0.203$ (M3, $[-0.43,-0.00]$); for $\log h_{loss}$, $-0.113$, $-0.404$ (M2, $[-0.79,-0.01]$)
and $-0.161$. Only two differences exclude zero and both are losses. Poor identification
attenuates but does not explain the result: restricting to the 30 well-identified participants
lifts baseline $\log h_{gain}$ only to $0.288$.

\subsection{Predictive Performance (Choice and RT, by Condition)}

\emph{RT.} On the run B RT predictive log-likelihood the three models separate
(Fig.~\ref{fig:prediction}, Table~\ref{tab:prediction}): M2 $-1.492$, M1 $-1.536$, M3 $-1.562$
nats per trial, M2 ahead of M1 ($p=0.040$) and M3 ($p=0.004$), M1 ahead of M3 ($p=0.008$). M2 is
nominally best in both conditions, has the lowest $\log$RT RMSE ($0.754$), and is the one
ranking here that generalises: on run A it already beat M1 ($p=0.005$) and M3 ($p=0.015$). Every
model still loses about $0.21$ nats per trial between runs, more than any difference between
models, because participants speed up by a median of $0.40$\,s while $\tau$ and $a$ are fixed
from run A.

\emph{Choice.} Carried across tasks the choice model performs \emph{below} chance
($\log0.5=-0.693$): the baseline reaches $-1.004$ nats per trial, 59.5\% balanced accuracy and
AUC $0.593$, with only 6 of 49 participants above chance, and does better on reward than loss
trials ($-0.897$ against $-1.111$).

\emph{Same-task control.} That collapse is a fact about the two tasks, not the model. Fitting
each participant's choice model on half of run A and predicting the other half -- held-out
trials, identical estimator, $\theta_{cert}\equiv0$ on both sides -- gives $-0.464$ nats per
trial and AUC $0.811$, with \textbf{49 of 49} participants above chance (paired gap $+0.493$,
$p=1.2\times10^{-12}$). With Section~3.1, this pins the failure: model and estimator work, and
what fails to transfer is a discount rate learned where the safe option is certain.

\subsection{Value of the RT Modality}

\emph{(a) Out-of-sample choice prediction: no reliable effect either way.} Against the baseline
the RT models sit at $\Delta=-0.037$ (M1, $p=0.35$), $+0.003$ (M2, $p=0.38$) and $+0.025$ (M3,
$p=0.29$) nats per trial, 23 to 26 of 49 participants improved (Fig.~\ref{fig:value}); balanced
accuracy falls slightly in all three and nothing approaches significance. Unlike the RT
comparison the direction is not even consistent: for the choices, modelling RT is a wash.

\emph{(b) Identifiability and reliability: RT helps in simulation, not in the data.} Simulation
says RT should help substantially, most under M3, and that gain survives a change of task
structure (\S3.1); on real data none of it appears, and the only two differences excluding zero
are losses (\S3.2). Effect size reconciles them: the simulation draws $b$ from ranges implying a
strong difficulty effect; the real one is near null. The within-participant slope of
$\log$RT on $|\Delta V|$ is $-0.027$ and the raw pooled slope $+0.063$, so even its sign depends
on how participant-level RT offsets are handled, and median fitted $b$ is weakly positive but
negative in 20, 11 and 16 of 49 participants, so the RT term injects variance into the shared
parameters rather than information. Modelling RT therefore buys an account of
response time itself, and under M2 a mapping that replicates across runs, but neither a better
account of the choices nor a more stable discount rate.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{We recommend M2, decision uncertainty.} On the quantity the brief designates for
separating mappings, the run B RT predictive log-likelihood, M2 is best at $-1.492$ nats per
trial against $-1.536$ and $-1.562$, and the tests separate all three ($p=0.040$, $0.004$,
$0.008$). It leads in both conditions and is the only ranking here that survives the move to a
structurally different run B. Psychologically that favours
saturation: what slows a participant is indecision, not value distance. The counterweight is
that M2 is the weakest of the three
at what RT was meant to buy us: its simulated recovery gain for $\log h_{gain}$ is nil (RMSE
ratio $0.98$ against M3's $0.47$) and it most damages the stability of $\log h_{loss}$
($\Delta r=-0.404$, CI $[-0.79,-0.01]$). We recommend it as the best description of response
time, not as an improvement to the discounting model.

\textbf{Was modelling RT worthwhile?} Not for the choices, where the three mappings move the
choice predictive log-likelihood by at most $0.037$ nats per trial in either direction, none
significant; and not for the constructs, where the large simulated recovery gains fail to
materialise. Model RT if response time is the object of study, not for a better discount rate.

\textbf{What we could not establish, and limitations.} We cannot claim any mapping is true: M2
wins by $0.04$--$0.07$ nats per trial against a between-run drop of $\approx0.20$ that hits every
model equally. Nor can we estimate a transferable discount rate, and that is structural, not a
modelling failure. $h$ recovers from run-B-structure designs ($r=0.82$--$0.93$), and the
same-task control predicts held-out run A choices at $-0.464$ nats per trial with 49 of 49
participants above chance, against $-1.004$ and 6 of 49 across tasks: model and estimator are
sound, transfer is what fails, and \S3.2 follows from it. Three limitations bound the rest. The
RT model is descriptive, not mechanistic: a shifted log-normal with a value-driven location
cannot separate drift from boundary, so a speed--accuracy shift and a change in valuation are
confounded in $b$, the likeliest reason RT can damage $h$. $\tau$ is unidentified, and the
floor applied to run B RTs below run A's $\tau$ is worth about $0.05$ nats per trial, the same
order as the gaps between RT models; M2 is also the least numerically stable model, a caveat we
state rather than hide about the model we recommend. Finally the model is deliberately simple:
estimation is per participant with no pooling and 19 of 49 are poorly identified, one $\beta$ is
shared across conditions, and $\Delta V$ is normalised so magnitude effects vanish by
construction.

\textbf{Next steps.} Fit a genuine sequential-sampling model, DDM or LBA, in which $\Delta V$
drives drift and a separate boundary carries speed--accuracy \citep{rodriguez2014,dai2014}: that
tests whether RT fails to sharpen the discount rates because the signal is absent or because our
functional form is wrong. Second, stop assuming one $h$ transfers: estimate a rate per task
structure and ask how the two relate.\label{lastbodypage}% the body must end on page 5 or earlier
